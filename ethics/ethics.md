# Design Ethics

**Date:** 09/10/2026  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Author:** Jonathan Smith

## Introduction

Ethics defines the principles that guide responsible, inclusive and trustworthy intelligent experiences.

These principles help teams make better judgements about how experiences should behave, particularly where technology may affect people’s rights, choices, privacy, safety or wellbeing.

They provide the ethical foundation for the standards in Basics and the practical techniques in Tactics.

---

### Design for inclusion by default
<small>IX-ETHIC-001</small>

Design experiences to work for as many people as possible from the outset, rather than treating accessibility and inclusion as later additions. Consider different abilities, contexts, devices, languages and levels of digital confidence.

### Be transparent about AI
<small>IX-ETHIC-002</small>

Make it clear when AI is being used, what role it plays and where its limitations may affect the experience. Give people enough information to understand when they are interacting with, or being influenced by, AI.

### Keep people informed and in control
<small>IX-ETHIC-003</small>

Help people understand consequential decisions, recommendations or actions that affect them, and provide meaningful opportunities to review, question or change them. Avoid removing human judgement or control where the potential impact is significant.

### Protect personal and sensitive information
<small>IX-ETHIC-004</small>

Collect, use and expose only the information necessary for the experience to function. Make privacy implications understandable, protect sensitive information throughout the journey, and avoid revealing personal data unexpectedly or without a clear need.

### Avoid manipulation and undue pressure
<small>IX-ETHIC-005</small>

Do not use patterns that mislead, coerce or pressure people into decisions they might not otherwise make. Present choices fairly, make consequences clear, and avoid exploiting urgency, uncertainty, defaults or behavioural biases for organisational advantage.

### Design for fairness and unintended outcomes
<small>IX-ETHIC-006</small>

Consider how decisions, recommendations and automated behaviour may affect different people or groups. Look for potential bias, exclusion and unintended consequences, and design safeguards that reduce harm rather than assuming the same experience works equally for everyone.

### Design for human agency
<small>IX-ETHIC-007</small>

Support people in making informed choices rather than steering them towards predetermined outcomes. Preserve meaningful control, allow people to change direction, and avoid making automated recommendations feel mandatory when alternatives exist.

### Make consequences understandable
<small>IX-ETHIC-008</small>

Help people understand the likely impact of important actions before they commit to them. Where outcomes are uncertain, irreversible or significant, communicate that clearly enough for people to make an informed decision.

### Design for appropriate trust
<small>IX-ETHIC-009</small>

Do not make systems appear more capable, certain, objective or authoritative than they are. Use language, behaviour and presentation that help people form an accurate understanding of what the experience can and cannot reliably do.

### Provide meaningful recourse
<small>IX-ETHIC-010</small>

Give people a reasonable way to challenge, correct or seek review of outcomes that materially affect them. Avoid creating experiences where automated decisions feel final, unexplained or impossible to contest.

### Minimise unnecessary dependency
<small>IX-ETHIC-011</small>

Design experiences to support people without encouraging avoidable reliance on the product or system. Do not exploit emotional attachment, uncertainty or repeated engagement where this could undermine independent judgement or wellbeing.

### Respect people’s attention
<small>IX-ETHIC-012</small>

Treat attention as a limited resource rather than something to maximise at any cost. Avoid unnecessary interruptions, engagement loops or prompts that compete for attention without providing proportionate value.

### Use data proportionately
<small>IX-ETHIC-013</small>

Use information in ways that are appropriate to the purpose, sensitivity and expectations of the experience. Avoid collecting, inferring or combining data simply because it is technically possible.

### Make uncertainty visible
<small>IX-ETHIC-014</small>

Communicate when information, predictions or recommendations are uncertain rather than presenting them as definitive. Help people distinguish between known facts, estimates, assumptions and generated content where that distinction matters.

### Design for safe failure
<small>IX-ETHIC-015</small>

Assume that systems, data and automated decisions can fail. Consider the consequences of errors in advance and design experiences so mistakes are detectable, recoverable and unlikely to cause disproportionate harm.

### Avoid deceptive anthropomorphism
<small>IX-ETHIC-016</small>

Do not encourage people to believe an AI system has human understanding, emotions, intentions or relationships that it does not possess. Use human-like interaction only where it improves usability without creating misleading expectations.

### Consider wider impacts
<small>IX-ETHIC-017</small>

Look beyond the immediate user journey when evaluating an experience. Consider potential effects on non-users, communities, workers, society and the environment where the design could create meaningful consequences.

### Be accountable for outcomes
<small>IX-ETHIC-018</small>

Treat responsibility for the experience as belonging to the people and organisations that design and deploy it, not to the technology itself. Ensure important decisions and harms can be traced to accountable human owners.

### Respect human dignity
<small>IX-ETHIC-019</small>

Design interactions that treat people with respect, particularly in sensitive, stressful or vulnerable situations. Avoid language, behaviour or automated treatment that dehumanises, humiliates, stereotypes or reduces people to scores, categories or data points.

### Use automation proportionately
<small>IX-ETHIC-020</small>

Match the level of automation to the importance, complexity and potential consequences of the task. Do not automate decisions simply because it is possible when human judgement, involvement or discretion would produce a more appropriate outcome.

### Respect meaningful consent
<small>IX-ETHIC-021</small>

Ensure people can make genuinely informed and voluntary choices about significant uses of their data, participation or actions. Avoid treating consent as meaningful when refusing is unnecessarily difficult or the consequences are obscured.

### Protect security and integrity
<small>IX-ETHIC-022</small>

Design experiences to safeguard people, their information and their actions from unauthorised access, interference or misuse. Consider security as part of the experience from the outset rather than relying solely on controls added later.

### Preserve authorship and ownership
<small>IX-ETHIC-023</small>

Respect people’s rights and expectations around content they create, provide or collaborate on. Make it clear where AI has contributed and avoid implying ownership, authorship or permission that has not legitimately been established.

### Respect social and contextual boundaries
<small>IX-ETHIC-024</small>

Consider whether behaviour that is acceptable in one context may be inappropriate in another. Design systems to recognise differences in audience, setting, sensitivity and relationship rather than applying the same behaviour indiscriminately.

### Avoid unnecessary surveillance
<small>IX-ETHIC-025</small>

Do not observe, track or infer more about people than is reasonably necessary for the experience. Consider the chilling effect that persistent monitoring can have even when the information collected is not obviously sensitive.

### Preserve human connection where it matters
<small>IX-ETHIC-026</small>

Do not replace meaningful human interaction purely for efficiency where empathy, judgement, responsibility or personal relationships are important to the outcome. Use technology to support human involvement rather than automatically displacing it.

### Make responsibility clear
<small>IX-ETHIC-027</small>

Help people understand who is responsible for a service, decision or outcome, particularly when several organisations, systems or automated processes are involved. Avoid experiences where accountability becomes obscured behind technology or organisational complexity.

### Design for long-term consequences
<small>IX-ETHIC-028</small>

Consider how an experience may affect people beyond the immediate interaction. Avoid optimising solely for short-term completion, engagement or organisational benefit when doing so could create foreseeable longer-term harm.

### Avoid unnecessary sensory demand
<small>IX-ETHIC-029</small>

Do not rely on excessive motion, sound, flashing, vibration or other sensory effects where they are not essential to the experience. Consider how stimulation may affect people with vestibular, neurological, sensory or cognitive sensitivities.

### Do not rely on one sense
<small>IX-ETHIC-030</small>

Ensure important information and meaning are not communicated through colour, sound, motion, shape or position alone. Design experiences so people can perceive and understand them through different combinations of senses and assistive technologies.

### Reduce avoidable cognitive burden
<small>IX-ETHIC-031</small>

Design experiences to minimise unnecessary memory, concentration and interpretation demands. Avoid complexity, ambiguity or interaction patterns that disproportionately disadvantage people with cognitive, learning, attention or neurological differences.

### Respect different interaction needs
<small>IX-ETHIC-032</small>

Do not assume everyone can interact quickly, precisely or continuously. Consider people who use keyboards, switches, voice input, assistive technology or alternative pointing methods, and avoid experiences that depend unnecessarily on speed or fine motor control.

### Give people control over stimulation
<small>IX-ETHIC-033</small>

Allow people to reduce, pause or avoid non-essential motion, animation, audio and other dynamic behaviour where it may cause discomfort, distraction or difficulty. Do not make sensory intensity a prerequisite for using the experience.

### Design for varying levels of ability
<small>IX-ETHIC-034</small>

Recognise that people’s abilities can be permanent, temporary, situational or change over time. Avoid designing around an assumed “average” user and consider how context, fatigue, injury or environment may affect someone’s ability to interact.

### Preserve access under constraint
<small>IX-ETHIC-035</small>

Design core tasks so they remain usable when people have limited vision, hearing, dexterity, attention, bandwidth, device capability or environmental control. Do not make access to essential functions depend on ideal conditions.

### Avoid accessibility trade-offs by default
<small>IX-ETHIC-036</small>

Do not improve convenience, aesthetics or engagement for some users by creating unnecessary barriers for others. Where trade-offs are unavoidable, prioritise access to essential information and actions.
